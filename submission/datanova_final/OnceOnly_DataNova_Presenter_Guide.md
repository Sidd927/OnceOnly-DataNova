## Slide by slide

### 1. Title: OnceOnly

**Timing:** 0:00-0:20 · 20 s

**Say:** Hi, I'm Siddhant, and this is OnceOnly. A carbon registry can prove that a credit was retired. What it can't tell you is how many public climate claims are quietly relying on those same retired units. OnceOnly checks exactly that, unit by unit, on real registry data, and shows its working for every finding.

**Why this slide matters:** Judges see many decks; the opening must state the problem in one sentence a non-specialist understands.

**Key point to leave with judges:** Retirement is checked once; claims about it are not checked at all.

**Transition:** Let me show you what that gap looks like on one real registry record.

### 2. Retirement is checked once. Claims on it are not.

**Timing:** 0:20-1:00 · 40 s

**Say:** Registries such as Verra and Gold Standard make sure a credit is retired only once. But retired units then travel into sustainability reports, product labels and net-zero statements, often through a broker who splits one retirement among several buyers. Here is a real Verra retirement from August 2026: 19,999 units, with no beneficiary disclosed. In our demo, two synthetic companies each publish a claim on that batch: 10,000 units and 7,000 units. Each claim checks out against the registry on its own. Even the totals look fine: 17,000 is less than 19,999. Yet 4,559 units are being claimed by both. Double counting is something the market's own integrity standard, the ICVCM's Core Carbon Principles, says must not happen.

**Why this slide matters:** Makes the gap concrete with a real record and shows why the obvious sanity check (totals) passes.

**Key point to leave with judges:** Each claim is valid alone; the problem only exists between claims.

**Transition:** So why don't existing checks catch this? Because of three properties of real registry data.

### 3. Real registry data defeats the obvious shortcuts

**Timing:** 1:00-1:40 · 40 s

**Say:** We measured three reasons on real data. First, overlapping claims rarely quote the same serial string or the same document, so matching strings or file hashes caught only a third of the double uses in our 270-pair test. Second, serial numbers restart inside every issuance batch. Across the full Berkeley database, ignoring the batch would create 159,511 false overlaps. Third, 62.7 percent of the retirements we analyse don't name a beneficiary at all, so you can't solve this by matching company names.

**Why this slide matters:** Covers Problem Relevance and Data Rigor: the difficulty is shown with measured numbers, not asserted.

**Key point to leave with judges:** The right unit of analysis is units inside a batch, with explicit handling of unknown beneficiaries.

**Transition:** That leads to the one idea the whole system is built on.

### 4. Turn every claim into unit intervals inside its batch

**Timing:** 1:40-2:20 · 40 s

**Say:** A registry serial isn't just an ID. It encodes a contiguous range of units inside one batch. This is Helios's serial: batch 18453, first unit 896,315,001, last unit 896,322,000. End minus start plus one is exactly 7,000, which matches the quantity Helios states. Once every claim is an interval inside its batch, double use becomes interval intersection. Below is the real registry retirement and the two claims drawn to scale. The overlap is exact: 896,315,001 to 896,319,559, 4,559 units. That works even when the two documents quote completely different serial strings.

**Why this slide matters:** This is the technical insight judges should remember; everything else builds on it.

**Key point to leave with judges:** Serials are ranges, not strings: double use = interval intersection within one batch.

**Transition:** Here is the pipeline we built around that idea.

### 5. One pipeline: claim document in, explained result out

**Timing:** 2:20-2:55 · 35 s

**Say:** OnceOnly is a working full-stack system. It reads the evidence and fingerprints every document. It reconciles each serial against real registry retirements. Then come the three stages I built for DATANOVA, highlighted here: entity resolution with allocation classification, population, timing and cross-registry analytics, and a unified assessment of 16 deterministic rules. Finally, accepted claims are anchored on a smart contract that refuses overlapping units, and anyone can verify them. The split matters: the analytics stay off-chain where they can be explained; only one invariant lives on-chain.

**Why this slide matters:** Technical Implementation is 25% of the score; this shows breadth and is honest about what was new for DATANOVA.

**Key point to leave with judges:** Off-chain: explainable analysis. On-chain: one enforced invariant.

**Transition:** Let me show you this running in the product.

### 6. 4,559 units already back another claim. Blocked.

**Timing:** 2:55-3:40 · 45 s

**Say:** This is the live application, not a mock-up. On Helios's claim page, the Ruler draws the real registry record and both claims on the same unit axis. One: the registry record is real, a Verra wind project in Tamil Nadu. Two: the overlap is computed exactly, 4,559 units, which is 65 percent of Helios's claim. Three: anchoring is blocked by the double-use rule, and if you send the registration to the chain anyway, the contract itself reverts with a RangeOverlap error. Note the wording: potential double use, for review. It doesn't say which company is right.

**Why this slide matters:** Proves a working product; ties the abstract idea to a real screen judges can open themselves.

**Key point to leave with judges:** It works end to end on a real record, and the contract enforces the same rule as the UI.

**Transition:** But an overlap on its own isn't enough to call something a problem.

### 7. An overlap is a question, not a verdict

**Timing:** 3:40-4:25 · 45 s

**Say:** Brokers and corporate groups share units legitimately all the time, so a naive overlap check would accuse innocent parties. OnceOnly combines the interval geometry with who the claimants are. Distinct ranges from one retirement are a legitimate split. A broker retiring on behalf of a disclosed client is an intermediary attribution. A parent and a subsidiary are related claimants, never treated as the same entity. If the beneficiary is undisclosed, we say it's unresolved instead of guessing. Only unrelated companies sharing units become an allocation conflict. Underneath sits entity resolution: legal-suffix normalisation, 'on behalf of' parsing and explainable matching. On our 124-pair curated benchmark it scores an F1 of 0.982 with zero false positives, against 0.263 for exact string matching. That benchmark is curated, so it shows the logic works as designed, not real-world accuracy.

**Why this slide matters:** Innovation and Analytical Rigor: this is what separates OnceOnly from a simple interval checker.

**Key point to leave with judges:** Geometry plus relationships, with 'unresolved' as an explicit honest answer.

**Transition:** Every one of these signals has to explain itself, which is the next layer.

### 8. Every signal shows its rule, its evidence and its limits

**Timing:** 4:25-5:00 · 35 s

**Say:** On top of that, a unified assessment runs 16 versioned, deterministic rules across six scopes: a batch, a project, a claim, an entity, a cross-registry pair, or an ad-hoc range. Every finding carries its rule ID, the threshold, the metrics it evaluated, supporting evidence, counterevidence, missing information and a recommended next step. Signals that come from the same underlying records are grouped, so three findings here count as two independent signals, not three. And the outputs are review priorities such as Review Recommended, never a fraud verdict. No model is trained here; this is deterministic and auditable by design.

**Why this slide matters:** Explainability is a differentiator for investigators and directly answers 'is this a black box?'.

**Key point to leave with judges:** Deterministic, versioned rules; correlated signals can't inflate priority; no fraud verdicts.

**Transition:** The same engine also describes the market population the claim sits in, on real data.

### 9. 37,335 real retirements: most name no beneficiary

**Timing:** 5:00-5:40 · 40 s

**Say:** Everything you've seen runs on a committed subset of the real Berkeley database: 22 Verra and Gold Standard projects, 725 batches, 37,335 retirements and 87.7 million tonnes, from 2010 to 2026. The headline is disclosure. 62.7 percent of retirement records, about 60 percent of the volume, don't name who the credits were retired for. That's exactly the gap where double claiming can hide, and it's why 'unresolved' is a first-class answer in OnceOnly. Among the disclosed volume, concentration is low: an HHI of 246. Sizes vary enormously across the portfolio, with a pooled Gini of 0.96, but much less inside a typical batch. And timing analysis flags 51 of 192 months as retirement bursts. These describe this sample, not the global market, and they're signals, not findings.

**Why this slide matters:** Data and Analytical Rigor (15%): real data, defined populations, and careful interpretation.

**Key point to leave with judges:** Real data, defined population, honest interpretation: disclosure is the structural blind spot.

**Transition:** Now, where does the blockchain come in, and where does it not?

### 10. One invariant on-chain. Everything else explainable.

**Timing:** 5:40-6:20 · 40 s

**Say:** We use a smart contract for one specific job. ClaimLedger stores each claim's unit ranges per batch, the evidence and report hashes, a salted claimant commitment and the status, and it refuses any registration that overlaps an active claim, even if someone bypasses our own interface. Documents and analytics never go on-chain. To keep that check cheap, ranges are kept in a sorted linked list and the client supplies a predecessor hint, so registration gas stays flat at about 301,000 whether a batch holds 10 or 200 ranges. A naive linear scan reached 833,000. To be precise about trust: in this prototype it's a local Hardhat chain with one trusted registrar. A hash match shows a document hasn't changed; it doesn't prove the document is true.

**Why this slide matters:** Pre-empts the most common skeptical question about blockchain in hackathon projects.

**Key point to leave with judges:** Blockchain = neutral ordering + a non-bypassable no-overlap rule. Not proof of truth.

**Transition:** So how do we know any of this actually works?

### 11. Tested for what each test can actually show

**Timing:** 6:20-7:00 · 40 s

**Say:** We validated each layer and labelled what each test can and can't show. On 270 synthetic claim pairs built on real Verra ranges, the engine and the contract both catch every double use and agree on every case, while hash or string matching catches only a third, and a batch-blind interval check produces false alarms. Because the ground truth there uses our own interval definition, it shows the logic is correct, not how common double claiming is. Entity resolution and allocation classification were tested on curated cases, including held-out ones. The engineering is tested too: 274 backend tests and 24 contract tests pass today, plus 45 browser tests and a 13-step end-to-end run from the release QA.

**Why this slide matters:** Technical Implementation and Rigor: evidence, with the honesty that makes it believable.

**Key point to leave with judges:** Measured against baselines, with the limits of each benchmark stated up front.

**Transition:** Which brings me to what this can't do yet, and where it goes next.

### 12. Honest about today, concrete about what comes next

**Timing:** 7:00-7:35 · 35 s

**Say:** Here's what OnceOnly doesn't do yet. The chain is local with a trusted registrar. The registry data is a static August 2026 snapshot, and the analytics cover 22 projects. The benchmarks are synthetic or curated, the optional AI extraction isn't evaluated, and we have not measured how often double claiming happens in the wild. The people it helps are buyers checking a claim before they rely on it, auditors who need a reviewable trail, and registries and researchers who want claim-level transparency. Next steps: claimant-signed registrations on a public testnet, per-document Merkle proofs, a refresh pipeline for the full registry, and a pilot on real published claims to measure prevalence.

**Why this slide matters:** Impact and Feasibility (15%) and credibility: stating limits first makes the impact claims believable.

**Key point to leave with judges:** Clear limits, clear users, a concrete roadmap.

**Transition:** To close.

### 13. Close: Every retired tonne, claimed once

**Timing:** 7:35-7:50 · 15 s

**Say:** So: work on units, not strings. Treat an overlap as a question, not a verdict. And put one invariant on-chain while keeping everything else explainable. Every retired tonne, claimed once. The demo is live, and the code and evidence are public. Thank you, I'm happy to take questions, or to walk through the live demo.

**Why this slide matters:** Leaves judges with three memorable takeaways and the links.

**Key point to leave with judges:** Units, not strings. Questions, not verdicts. One invariant on-chain.
